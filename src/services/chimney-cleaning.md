## Why it matters: creosote and fire risk

When wood burns, smoke carries unburned particles and vapors up the chimney. As they cool, they condense on the flue walls as **creosote**. It starts as a flaky, soot-like layer and can harden into a shiny, tar-like glaze. Every form of it is combustible.

If enough builds up and a hot fire reaches it, the creosote can ignite inside the flue. That's a chimney fire. Some are loud and dramatic, but many are slow-burning and go unnoticed, and they can still crack flue liners and damage the masonry. Regular cleaning keeps buildup from reaching that point.

Creosote builds faster when:

- You burn unseasoned or wet wood
- Fires are kept small and smoldering, with restricted air
- The flue is oversized for the appliance or runs cold (common on exterior chimneys)
- Your stove or insert is vented through a flue that wasn't sized for it

## How often should a chimney be cleaned?

The fire code standard **NFPA 211** calls for an inspection once a year, with cleaning done when it's needed. How often that is depends on how much you burn and what you burn. A household with a fire most winter evenings will need cleaning more often than one that lights a few fires a year. Your annual inspection answers the question for your home.

## Signs it's time to schedule

- It's been more than a year since your last cleaning or inspection
- A strong, smoky or tar-like smell from the fireplace, especially on hot or humid days
- Smoke drifting into the room instead of drawing up the chimney
- Black flakes or debris falling into the firebox
- Fires that are harder to start or keep going
